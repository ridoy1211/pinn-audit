Comparative Analysis of Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Classical Methods for Inverse Heat Transfer Problems
Executive Summary
The identification of thermal properties and initial states in heat conduction systems represents a fundamental class of inverse problems with applications ranging from materials science to biomedical imaging. Traditionally, these problems have been addressed using classical numerical techniques such as the Finite Element Method (FEM) and Finite Difference Methods (FDM), often augmented with explicit regularization strategies like Tikhonov regularization to mitigate ill-posedness. In recent years, Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML), and specifically Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), has emerged as a disruptive alternative, promising to solve inverse problems without the need for explicit mesh generation or handcrafted regularizers.

This literature review evaluates the current state of research across five key studies involving heat inversion, parameter identification, and comparative benchmarking of neural versus classical procedures. The review highlights that while PINNs and their variants (e.g., Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks, Multiscale PINNs) offer significant flexibility and scaling advantages for complex geometries and high-dimensional spaces, they frequently struggle to match the accuracy and computational efficiency of traditional methods in low-dimensional, well-defined scenarios. Specifically, studies indicate that traditional FEM-based optimizers can outperform PINNs by orders of magnitude in accuracy, particularly in the presence of noise.

A critical research gap is identified regarding the joint inversion of constant diffusivity and unknown initial fields. While individual studies address these components separately, there is a lack of explicit comparative data on how the relative performance of neural and classical methods shifts when initial-state regularization, basis capacity (network architecture), and neural optimizer budgets are systematically varied and made explicit. This report justifies the existence of this gap and provides recommendations for future research to address these multi-factor interactions.

1. Introduction
Inverse problems in partial differential equations (PDEs) involve the recovery of unknown parameters, initial conditions, or boundary values from observed data of the system's state. In the context of the heat equation—a parabolic PDE describing thermal diffusion—these problems are notoriously ill-posed, meaning that small errors in observed measurements can lead to disproportionately large errors in the reconstructed solution [2, 6].

For decades, the standard approach has relied on classical discretization methods (FEM, FDM) combined with numerical optimization and explicit regularization to stabilize the inversion [1, 5, 8]. However, the rise of deep learning has introduced Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which embed the governing physical laws directly into the loss function of a neural network [4, 7, 8]. This allows the network to act as a universal function approximator that naturally respects the underlying physics, potentially reducing the need for explicit regularization and mesh-based constraints [2, 6].

This report reviews five specific papers to assess the comparative strengths of these paradigms, focusing on their application to heat transfer and diffusion problems. It further investigates a specific research question regarding the interaction of regularization, network capacity, and optimizer budget in joint inversion tasks.

Classical theory emphasizes that backward heat transfer—reconstructing the initial state from a final state—is inherently unstable because the heat operator is a smoothing operator that dampens high-frequency components of the initial field over time. Consequently, regularization techniques are essential in classical frameworks to find a balance between data fidelity and solution smoothness [1, 6].

3. Methodological Approaches in the Literature
3.1 Classical Numerical Methods and Regularization
Traditional approaches to heat inversion typically employ the Finite Element Method (FEM) or Finite Difference Method (FDM) to discretize the forward problem. Identification of parameters then proceeds via a numerical optimizer (e.g., L-BFGS or Levenberg-Marquardt) that minimizes the discrepancy between simulated and observed data [5, 8, 9]. To handle ill-posedness, methods like Tikhonov regularization, entropy-based regularization, or variational approaches are frequently used [2, 6].

3.3 Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) and Multiscale Approaches
Recent developments have introduced variations such as Multiscale PINNs and Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) to improve performance. KANs, in particular, replace the fixed linear weights of standard neural networks with learnable univariate functions (B-splines) on the edges of the network [2, 6]. Oliveira et al. [2, 6] demonstrate that KANs and PINNs can reconstruct initial conditions in heat conduction problems without explicit regularization, effectively utilizing the PDE constraint as an implicit regularizer. KANs are highlighted for their ability to achieve high-resolution solutions by adapting the basis capacity through the B-spline representations [2, 6].

4. Comparative Analysis of Findings
4.1 Parameter Recovery and Diffusion Estimation
Several studies focus on the estimation of the diffusion coefficient. Zapf et al. [4, 7] apply PINNs and FEM to estimate molecular transport (diffusion) in the human brain from MRI data. They found that for PINNs to achieve accurate parameter recovery, the PDE residual after training must be extremely small. This often requires careful tuning of weights in the loss function and the use of residual-based adaptive refinement of training points. Their results show that PINN estimates become consistent with FEM-based approaches only after such meticulous optimization [4, 7].

4.2 Initial Condition Reconstruction (Backward Heat Transfer)
The "backward heat problem" is specifically addressed by Oliveira et al. [2, 6]. They compare standard PINNs, Multiscale PINNs, and KANs for initial condition estimation. Their findings suggest that these PIML formulations can accurately reconstruct initial distributions even in the presence of noise, without requiring the explicit Tikhonov-style regularization typically mandated in classical procedures [2, 6]. Standard PINNs sometimes struggle with spectral bias, where they fail to capture high-frequency components of the solution, a problem partially mitigated by multiscale architectures [2, 6].

4.3 Accuracy and Efficiency Trade-offs
A recurring theme in the literature is the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost. Grossmann et al. [3, 9] systematically compare PINNs and FEM across various PDEs (Poisson, Allen-Cahn, Schrödinger). Their results indicate that PINNs generally fail to outperform FEM in terms of both solution time and absolute accuracy for standard problems. While PINNs were faster at evaluating the solved PDE, the training process was significantly more resource-intensive than the FEM solver [3, 9, 68].

Jekic et al. [5, 8] reinforce this finding in the context of inverse problems. In viscosity identification for fluid flow (Burgers' equation and Taylor-Green Vortex), traditional FEM-based optimizers achieved significantly lower Root Mean Square Errors (RMSE) than PINNs. For example, in a 2D Taylor-Green Vortex with specific noise levels, the baseline FEM approach yielded an RMSE of 0.0013, whereas the best PINN variation only achieved 0.01 [5, 8].

4.4 Robustness to Noise and Measurement Error
The robustness of neural versus classical methods to noisy data is a point of contention. While PINNs are often claimed to be robust, Jekic et al. [5, 8] found that PINNs are generally outperformed by traditional methods when Gaussian noise is applied to training and validation data. Conversely, Oliveira et al. [2, 6] suggest that PINNs and KANs maintain high-resolution solutions for heat inversion even with noise, attributing this to the stabilizing effect of the physics constraint in the loss function. Zapf et al. [4, 7] also note that the standard PINN formulation faces challenges with noisy MRI measurements, requiring

5. Limitations of the Current Evidence
The reviewed literature reveals several limitations:

Optimizer Pathologies: Training PINNs is a non-convex optimization problem prone to gradient pathologies and local minima, which can lead to failure in parameter recovery [4, 5, 7, 8].
Hyperparameter Sensitivity: The accuracy of PINNs is highly dependent on the weighting of loss terms, network architecture, and the choice of collocation points [4, 7].
Scope of Comparison: Most studies compare PINNs to a "standard" baseline without exploring how changes in the baseline's regularization or the PINN's optimizer budget might alter the comparative ranking [5, 8, 9].

Conclusion on the Gap
The papers individually address parts of the problem:

Paper 2 & 6: Address initial condition recovery and basis capacity (KAN vs PINN) for heat transfer.
Paper 3 & 9: Address accuracy/efficiency (budget) for forward problems and some benchmarks.
Paper 4 & 7: Address diffusivity recovery and the need for small residuals (tuning/budget) in biological diffusion.
Paper 5 & 8: Address noise robustness and demonstrate that classical methods often win on accuracy for fluid inverse problems.
However, the specific "how" in the user's question—the functional relationship between the neural-classical performance gap and the trio of variables (regularization, capacity, budget) for joint heat inversion—is not answered. The evidence suggests that traditional methods are currently superior in accuracy for low-dimensional heat problems [5, 8], but the crossover point where PINNs might become superior (e.g., at higher basis capacity or specific optimizer budgets) remains unquantified in these sources.

7. Conclusion
The integration of physics into neural networks offers a powerful tool for solving inverse heat transfer problems, particularly where mesh generation is difficult or dimensions are high. However, for joint constant-diffusivity and unknown-initial-field inversion, classical methods like FEM-based optimization remain more accurate and efficient in the scenarios studied thus far [3, 5, 8, 9]. The transition of PINNs from a promising alternative to a superior procedure depends on overcoming optimization challenges and a deeper understanding of how network capacity and training budgets interact with physical constraints. The specific multi-factor comparison requested by the user remains an open research question, representing a significant gap in the current literature.
